\title{Evidence-Gated Autonomy: A Synthetic Benchmark for Consequential Action Authorization Under Uncertain and Compromised Evidence}

\begin{abstract}
As AI systems are given greater autonomy, a central deployment question is not only whether a model can produce a plausible answer, but whether the available evidence is sufficient to authorize a consequential action. This work introduces Evidence-Gated Autonomy (EGA), a synthetic benchmark and action-authorization framework that maps observable evidence states to ACT, VERIFY, REQUEST EVIDENCE, DEFER, or ESCALATE. The benchmark contains 500 underlying evidence situations paired across low- and high-consequence actions, producing 1,000 cases across ten controlled failure families. In an idealized deterministic testbed, EGA produced 0/500 unsafe high-consequence ACT decisions, compared with 50/500 for tested Risk-Gate and Always-Verify baselines. Under stochastic verification, retrieval, and escalation, high-risk incorrect-action rate increased from 1.86\% in an optimistic regime to 6.10\% in a moderate regime and 13.09\% in a harsh regime. Adversarial tests showed that spoofed dependency metadata and masked external evidence could defeat the original gate. Ed25519 provenance attestations prevented direct unsafe ACTs under several unsigned metadata attacks, but valid signatures over false claims remained dangerous under trust-root compromise. A dual-lineage separation-of-duties design raised the compromise threshold for fabricated evidence independence. A subsequent four-authority stress test on benchmark v0.4 found 50 unsafe ACT decisions under witness-authority compromise alone, 300 under capture-plus-witness or key-administration trust-store compromise, and 600 under compromise of the final authorization authority. A separate authenticated witness-quorum sensitivity study added bounded recovery for 50 safe verifier-false-reject cases: the honest baseline reached 100/100 safe-action coverage, but 2-of-3 still admitted 50/700 unsafe actions under two-witness collusion and registry-root compromise; 3-of-3 blocked those modeled cases but not full-roster compromise and failed closed when one credential was invalid. These are deterministic synthetic outcomes under explicit capabilities, not real-world probabilities. All current results are synthetic or simulated and do not establish production safety.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Increasingly capable AI systems are being integrated into workflows where model outputs can trigger external actions. The central question studied here is: what evidence should an AI system require before it is allowed to take the next action?

Evidence-Gated Autonomy treats action authorization as a structured evidence problem. The controller considers evidence completeness, cross-document consistency, freshness, external grounding, verifier disagreement, source dependence, action consequence, and later, cryptographically bound provenance.

The contribution is a reproducible synthetic benchmark, comparative policy evaluation, adversarial falsification tests, and provenance/trust-root experiments. The work does not claim to solve autonomous-agent safety.

\section{Related Work}
EGA is adjacent to AI control and scalable oversight, which study protocols for supervising powerful or potentially untrusted models \cite{greenblatt2024aicontrol,kenton2024scalableoversight,jiang2026disagreement,holland2026debate}. Reject-option and selective-prediction research studies the coverage--risk trade-off when a system may abstain, while prover-verifier deliberation examines whether a separate verifier can improve selective prediction \cite{hendrickx2024rejectoption,sedoc2026trustverify}. These lines of work motivate abstention and escalation, but do not by themselves establish that the evidence supplied to a verifier is authentic, fresh, correctly scoped, or independent.

Recent work directly studies common-cause failures in agentic verification. He and Yu introduce Epistemic Fault Domains and dependency-aware quorum admission, including structural analyses of when apparently independent agents share evidence ancestry \cite{he2026illusionquorums}. VP-CONTROL studies cost-aware verification portfolios under common-mode data failures and commit-gate enforcement \cite{zheng2026vpcontrol}. These papers substantially overlap with the broad premise that agreement among multiple agents is not sufficient when their evidence dependencies are shared. Accordingly, this paper does not claim a new quorum theory or a general solution to correlated agent failures; the v3 quorum study is treated as a synthetic implementation and threshold-sensitivity experiment.

The EXIM application area also has relevant prior work. TradeSense-EQA combines trade declarations with physical sensing (including weighing, RFID, GPS/AIS and electronic seals), evidence chains, cross-modal risk analysis and abstention \cite{chen2026tradesenseeqa}. Earlier work studies data-driven fraud detection in international shipping and machine-learning assessment of HS-code correctness in customs declarations \cite{triepels2018shippingfraud,chen2021hscode}. EGA does not claim to introduce multimodal trade sensing or trade-document anomaly detection, and its synthetic benchmark is not a reproduction of TradeSense-EQA's data or model.

A separate literature addresses authorization at the execution boundary. Work on execution-boundary conformance, runtime proof of execution, commit-time authorization and agentic transaction processing binds evidence or authority to the action that is ultimately committed \cite{wu2026executiongrant,rhodes2026proofexecution,santosgrueiro2026temporaryauthority,chang2026mnemosyne}. These systems overlap with EGA's general action-gating motivation. The present experiments therefore do not claim generic execution gating as novel; they investigate how evidence-chain failures and trust assumptions affect a synthetic document-driven controller.

The provenance component is informed by established software-supply-chain and IoT assurance systems. in-toto provides signed statements about software supply-chain history, while SLSA defines verifiable provenance describing where and how artifacts were produced \cite{torresarias2019intoto,slsa2026}. Surveys of data provenance in security and IoT, and device-integrity frameworks such as IoTAttest, show that cryptographic provenance and remote attestation are established research areas \cite{pan2023dataprovenance,faraj2025iotprovenance,dirin2023iotattest}. Capture-time authenticity and multi-observer proof-of-location further show that device signatures alone may not establish that a recorded physical event actually occurred \cite{brito2026captureauthenticity}. EGA's security experiments are an application-specific synthetic threat study, not a new cryptographic primitive or general provenance framework.

The key distinction tested here is between integrity/authenticity and semantic truth. A valid signature can establish that an enrolled key signed a particular claim and can protect that claim from undetected modification; it cannot alone establish that the source document was truthful, the sensor was calibrated, the signer was uncompromised, or the source was operationally independent. The contribution is therefore framed as a controlled benchmark and adversarial evaluation of evidence-gated action authorization under explicit assumptions, rather than a claim to solve autonomous-agent safety.

\section{Benchmark}
The benchmark is generated deterministically with seed 42 and contains 500 unique underlying evidence situations across ten controlled failure families. Each underlying state is paired with a LOW- and HIGH-consequence action, producing 1,000 total cases.

The ten families are clean, extraction error, cross-document conflict, missing critical evidence, consistent-but-wrong evidence, stale evidence, verifier false accept, verifier false reject, correlated verifier failure, and cascading upstream error.

Observable information includes document fields, authorization presence and freshness, externally measured weight, extraction outputs and confidence, verifier decisions, verifier dependency groups, and action consequence. Ground-truth and injected-fault labels are used only for evaluation.

\section{Action Policies}
The primary controller outputs ACT, VERIFY, REQUEST EVIDENCE, DEFER, or ESCALATE. For high-consequence actions it checks critical evidence presence, authorization freshness, cross-document agreement, external conflicts, extraction agreement, verifier rejection, and verifier dependency diversity.

Seven comparison policies are evaluated: Always Act, Confidence Gate, Document Agreement, Always Verify, Selective Abstention, Weak/Strong, and Risk Gate.

\section{Metric}
The primary metric is high-risk incorrect consequential action rate (ICAR), the proportion of HIGH-risk cases that ultimately result in an incorrect consequential action. Additional metrics include false authorization, false blocking, action rate, intervention rates, and normalized intervention cost.

\section{Experiment 1A: Deterministic Comparison}
Evidence Gate produced 0/500 incorrect HIGH-risk actions. Risk Gate and Always Verify each produced 50/500.

\section{Experiment 1B: Imperfect Verification and Escalation}
Across 30 Monte Carlo seeds per regime, Evidence Gate mean HIGH-risk ICAR was $0.0186 \pm 0.0052$ under optimistic assumptions, $0.0610 \pm 0.0115$ under moderate assumptions, and $0.1309 \pm 0.0131$ under harsh assumptions. Under the moderate regime, Risk Gate ICAR was approximately 0.1655.

\section{Ablations and Negative Results}
External grounding, source/dependency independence, and freshness provided measurable protection. Explicit verifier-status handling did not independently reduce deterministic ICAR after other checks were present. Risk conditioning primarily changed coverage and cost, while REQUEST EVIDENCE improved resolution and coverage without establishing intrinsic safety benefit.

\section{Experiment 1C: Provenance Stress}
At simulated 90\% human-review accuracy, Evidence Gate ICAR was approximately 6.4\% without attack, 15.36\% under dependency spoofing, 15.70\% under external-evidence masking, and 24.06\% under combined metadata spoofing. These tests invalidated the assumption that self-asserted provenance and dependency metadata was a sufficient trust foundation.

\section{Experiment 1D: Distribution Shift}
Under compound shift, Evidence Gate ICAR was 0.1195, Risk Gate 0.1851, and Confidence-only 0.7660. Provenance dropout increased Evidence Gate ICAR from approximately 0.0619 in-distribution to 0.0895.

\section{Experiment 2: Attested Provenance}
Experiment 2 generated 3,675 synthetic Ed25519 attestations binding provenance claims to current evidence objects. Under individual dependency, external-evidence, and timestamp spoofing attacks, Evidence Gate v1 reached 10\% deterministic ICAR while attestation-aware v2 produced 0\% direct unsafe ACT. Under combined spoofing, v1 reached 30\% and v2 0\% direct unsafe ACT, largely by escalating detected inconsistencies.

With simulated 90\%-accurate escalation, v2 still produced roughly 6--7\% ICAR under several detected-tamper conditions.

\section{Trust-Root Compromise}
Compromising a trusted verifier, external-sensor signer, or authorization signer reintroduced a 10\% deterministic failure family; compromising all relevant roots produced 30\%. A valid signature therefore establishes authenticity and integrity of a claim, not its truth.

\section{Separation of Duties}
A third design separates verifier-output authority from dependency-lineage authority. The verifier signs its output while two independent lineage authorities attest dependency lineage. Deterministic ICAR remained at 0\% under verifier-only, either single-lineage, or verifier plus one-lineage compromise. Compromising both lineage authorities restored 10\% ICAR. With imperfect escalation, single-authority compromise still produced approximately 7\% error and both-lineage compromise approximately 15\%.

\section{Discussion}
The experiments shift the central question from evidence quantity to evidence structure and trust. Multiple agreeing model outputs need not provide multiple independent reasons for action. External grounding can expose mutually consistent but incorrect evidence, yet its benefit collapses if that evidence can be hidden. Signed provenance can prevent undetected modification but cannot guarantee truth when trusted signers are compromised. Escalation quality is part of the safety system rather than an external assumption.

\section{Governance Relevance}
For regulators, auditors, standards bodies, and independent evaluators, the results motivate questions about what evidence supports a safety claim, whether checks are actually independent, who controls trust roots, how negative results are disclosed, and whether escalation effectiveness has been empirically measured.

The paper does not prescribe a regulatory regime. It motivates treating provenance, independence, and correlated failure as first-class properties of assurance claims for consequential AI deployments.

\section{Limitations}
All current performance results are synthetic or Monte Carlo simulations. There is no external frontier-model batch evaluation, real customs dataset, production deployment, real cryptographic infrastructure, or human-subject result. Cost units are normalized utilities. Trust-root compromise scenarios are stylized. The benchmark can establish controlled counterexamples and comparative proof-of-concept behavior, not deployment safety. In addition, the original source code and complete parameter configuration for the historical Experiments 1B--2E were not committed with their result tables. A separately versioned reconstruction protocol now runs under explicitly stated assumptions, but its outputs are not reproductions of the historical numbers. The original Experiment 1A downstream-resolution implementation is also absent; therefore the numerical tables in this manuscript remain historical reported outputs pending reconciliation with a fully recoverable implementation.

\section{Future Work}
A frozen real-model protocol will replace simulated extraction and verifier error with actual model outputs from independently developed model families. A preregistered human-oversight study will compare raw escalation output against structured evidence packets. Neither study has been run.

\section{Reproducibility}
Code, benchmark generation, results, claim ledgers, and preregistered protocols are available at:
\url{https://github.com/Hafiz-IIT/EXIM-AI-Evidence-Gated-Autonomy}

\section{Conclusion}
The strongest defensible conclusion is narrow: action authorization should reason not only about model outputs, but about the provenance, independence, freshness, external grounding, and failure structure of the evidence supporting those outputs. Whether such mechanisms improve real-world AI safety remains an empirical question.


\section{Supplementary Reconstruction: Document-Root Attestation on Benchmark v0.4}

This section reports a subsequent synthetic extension and is not merged into the historical v0.3 benchmark tables or original Experiment 2 results. Benchmark v0.4 contains 700 underlying situations paired across LOW/HIGH actions, for 1,400 cases and 14 fault families. The document-root threat runner evaluates the 700 HIGH-risk cases.

The document-root attestation signs canonical hashes of raw document instances and normalized document slots, together with tenant and case identifiers, signer/key identity, issuance and expiry times, and a nonce. Verification checks the trusted-key registry, revocation state, tenant/case scope, validity window, content digests, nonce replay state, and Ed25519 signature. The experiment uses deterministic test-only keys and process-local replay state.

The full CI run \href{https://github.com/Hafiz-IIT/EXIM-AI-Evidence-Gated-Autonomy/actions/runs/43955044311}{43955044311} passed regression tests, benchmark generation and integrity checks, v1/v2 reconstruction, the document-root runner, artifact uploads, historical-table loading, and manuscript compilation. Each of the nine document-root scenarios contains 700 HIGH-risk cases.

In the combined compromise scenario, the attacker is assumed to possess the trusted document-root private key and coordinate changes to raw documents, normalized document slots, machine extraction, HS classification, and externally measured weight. The forged attestations then verify for all 700 cases. The existing gate authorizes 350 ACT decisions, 300 unsafe relative to the original benchmark ground truth: 42.86\% of all HIGH-risk cases and 85.71\% of ACT decisions in this synthetic scenario.

The 300 unsafe ACTs occur in six families: C1 extraction error, C2 cross-document conflict, C4 consistent but wrong, C6 verifier false accept, C9 cascading error, and C13 corrupted provenance (50 each). The other 50 ACT decisions occur in the clean family and are safe. These are conditional outcomes under an intentionally strong combined-compromise assumption, not estimates of real-world attack probability and not results about document-root key theft alone.

The experiment demonstrates that a signature authenticates a payload relative to a key; it does not establish semantic truth when the signing authority and corroborating upstream channels are jointly compromised. The architectural implication is to separate independent capture attestation from extraction/normalization, external measurement, and action authorization. The capture authority should attest to source identity, acquisition channel, receipt time, and immutable raw-object digest, not certify that extracted values are true. High-consequence fields require corroboration from independently acquired sources and independently administered trust domains. A subsequent synthetic prototype implements four role-separated attestations for capture, interpretation, independent witness, and authorization, with a separate key-administration trust-store threat control. Its protocol and results are documented in the repository's evidence-trust v2 experiment. It does not implement production key management or authenticate real source independence.

The prototype uses deterministic test keys and process-local replay state. KMS/HSM-backed key custody, authenticated signer enrollment, durable atomic replay state, operational revocation, audit logging, secure capture and incident response are not implemented. Protocol and family-level results are documented in the repository's \href{https://github.com/Hafiz-IIT/EXIM-AI-Evidence-Gated-Autonomy/blob/main/experiments/document_root_attestation/RESULTS_REVIEW.md}{document-root results review}. The manuscript remains a synthetic research artifact and is not ready for arXiv submission.



\section{Supplementary Experiment: Four-Authority Evidence-Trust Architecture on Benchmark v0.4}

This subsequent experiment is separate from the historical v0.3 benchmark tables and original Experiments 1A--2E. It evaluates all 700 HIGH-risk v0.4 cases under the 15 non-empty compromise combinations of four roles: capture authority (CA), interpretation authority (IA), independent witness authority (IWA), and authorization authority (AA). An honest baseline and three separate controls bring the total to 19 scenarios and 13,300 case-scenario rows.

The prototype uses role-scoped Ed25519 attestations with tenant/case binding, parent digests and validity windows. The authorization harness checks raw-document digest binding, interpretation lineage, declared source/acquisition/administrative-domain separation, witness agreement, and the existing \texttt{evidence\_gate\_v1} policy. The independent witness is an idealized synthetic record derived from benchmark ground truth. The experiment is deterministic and does not estimate Monte Carlo uncertainty.

Interpretation + witness & 0 & 0 & 0\% & --\\

The witness-only compromise result is concentrated in C4 (consistent but wrong): 50 of 50 cases produce unsafe ACT. Capture + witness, capture + interpretation + witness, and key-admin trust-store poisoning each produce 300 unsafe ACTs across C1, C2, C4, C6, C9 and C13 (50 per family), plus 50 safe ACTs in C0. Any scenario containing AA produces 700 ACT decisions, of which 600 are unsafe. This is an explicit modeled consequence of compromising the component that enforces final authorization.

The source-forgery-before-capture control is blocked because the independent synthetic witness retains the ground-truth value. The disclosed shared-dependency control is blocked because the witness declares the same source identifier and acquisition/administrative domains as capture. This does not prove real-world independence: the prototype checks declared labels but does not authenticate domain ownership. A compromised IWA can falsify signed witness claims and assert an apparently independent source. The key-admin control models trust-store poisoning that enrolls attacker-controlled CA, IA and IWA keys while AA remains honest; it produces the same 300 unsafe ACT outcome as coordinated upstream-role compromise.

These results identify both the value and limits of role separation. Capture-only and interpretation-only compromise produced no unsafe ACT but blocked all ground-truth-safe cases in this benchmark. A single witness compromise produced unsafe ACT; coordinated upstream compromise recreated the v1 failure scale; AA compromise bypassed the gate entirely. The architecture therefore remains conditional on independent witness integrity, protected signer enrollment, and an authorization boundary that cannot be controlled by the attacker.
